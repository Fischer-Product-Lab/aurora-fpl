Most AI agent demos show the happy path: ask a question, get an answer. Aurora tests what happens when the work goes wrong.

Think of it as air-traffic control for a team of digital specialists. During a pretend flash-sale outage, four specialists check system health, a software release, payments, and security. They add their findings to a shared record. A planner recommends a response. An independent reviewer looks for gaps. Approval, budget, and health checks decide whether any action is allowed, and whether it actually worked.

The dashboard shows three controlled experiments. Independent review repaired all thirty incomplete plans. When one specialist was unavailable, one approved backup recovered all thirty runs. And when recovery needed four budget units but only three remained, every run stopped before work began, without spending anything.

Choose a scenario. Follow the plain-language story, or open the timeline and evidence to see how the decision was made.

Aurora is a laboratory, not a production claim. Its published studies are scripted and repeatable. The system can also accept one optional model-generated diagnosis through the same rules, while approval and execution remain controlled.

The idea is simple: reliable AI is not just a smart model. It is a well-governed system around it.
